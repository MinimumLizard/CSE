Group Chief Executive Officer’s Review - Interim Financial Statements


Dear stakeholder,

I am pleased to present the Quarterly Financial Review of Capital Alliance Holdings PLC (CALH) for
the period ended 30th September 2026.

The Group recorded a loss of LKR 344mn for the quarter, of which LKR 240mn is attributable to the
shareholders of CALH. This translates to a loss of LKR 0.12 per share. The loss in the past quarter is
entirely attributable to the primary dealing business vertical of CAL (CALT-which CALH owns 86%
of). Excluding CALT, the group has recorded a profit before tax of LKR 1.5bn and net profit of LKR
1.1bn for the first half of FY27.

Global capital markets have been rocked by the war in the Middle East this year. Sri Lanka and
Bangladesh – both net energy importers – were hit particularly hard as crude oil prices effectively doubled
from the beginning of the year, peaking at around USD 120 per barrel, and crack spreads (the premium
of refined products such as petrol and diesel over crude oil) widened from a pre-war average of less than
USD 20 per barrel to over USD 50 per barrel. Despite Sri Lanka’s strong macro fundamentals, the Central
Bank of Sri Lanka (CBSL) acted early and aggressively, raising policy rates by 100 basis points (bps) in
May. The move broadly achieved its objectives, with weaker demand and slower credit growth averting
any further weakening of the rupee. Capital markets were still adjusting to these changes at 30th
September 2026: average daily turnover on the Colombo Stock Exchange (CSE) had declined to around
LKR 1.5bn, and yields on Government securities were between 100 and 200bps higher than a year earlier.

At the time of writing, capital markets have rebounded significantly following the US midterm elections,
the outcome of which has raised hopes of a de-escalation in the Middle East. Crude oil prices have
declined by close to 20% from their late-October levels. The CSE has rebounded by close to 10%, with
turnover recovering to more ‘normal’ levels. Yields on Sri Lankan Government bonds have fallen by as
much as 80bps from their early-November peak, and now stand marginally below their levels at the end
of the quarter. The effect of the US elections has been more muted in Bangladesh, but the direction has
been the same. However, the geopolitical situation remains volatile, and the likelihood of a further flare-
up in the Middle East remains high.

Counterbalancing these headwinds has been the domestic stability in Sri Lanka. In September, Fitch
became the first of the major rating agencies to upgrade Sri Lanka’s sovereign credit rating from CCC+
to B-. There remains a high possibility of further rating upgrades, and of Sri Lanka gradually regaining
access to global capital markets. While the short term remains volatile and difficult to predict – and the
recovery in Bangladesh is taking longer than we had anticipated – we firmly believe that the longer-term
trajectory for both Sri Lanka and Bangladesh remains strong. Sri Lanka, in particular, appears to be
positioned for a long-term decline in interest rates over the next three to four years – something all our
businesses, from primary dealing to asset management and stockbroking, can benefit from.

Apart from primary dealing, CAL’s other business units have, on balance, performed well during this
financial year. Despite the geopolitical headwinds, operating income from non-primary dealing
operations increased by 12% year-on-year to LKR 2.5bn. Investment banking fee income rose to LKR
241mn, from LKR 89mn a year ago, bearing out the strong pipeline I referred to last quarter. Asset
management fee income grew by 18% to LKR 983mn. CAL private equity investments which leverages
the Group’s balance sheet, outside the primary dealing business, achieved a profit of LKR 575 mn for
the first half of the year. Stockbroking remained subdued during Q2 with turnover on the CSE falling to
its lowest levels of the year. This segment recorded a profit before tax of LKR 186mn.

On 2nd November 2026, CAL – through its Singapore subsidiary, CAL Global Pte Ltd – signed a share
purchase agreement to acquire 51% of Asia Frontier Capital (AFC), a Hong Kong-based asset manager
that focuses on frontier markets. Completion is subject to conditions precedent and regulatory approvals,
including that of the Securities and Futures Commission of Hong Kong. The transaction has been many
months in the making and, to our knowledge, marks the first time a Sri Lankan firm has moved to acquire
an international asset manager. It is a significant step in our strategy of overseas expansion.

There are clear synergies between CAL and AFC. With over USD 1.2bn in assets under management
(AUM), CAL adds scale to AFC, and muscle in terms of people, processes and technology. AFC, through
its Cayman Islands-based fund management platform, gives CAL a way to tap global capital and match
it with opportunities in Sri Lanka, Bangladesh and other frontier markets. While the entirety of AFC’s
USD 125mn in AUM is currently invested in listed equities, the company has a platform and the
necessary infrastructure to channel global capital into a range of asset classes in frontier markets,
including fixed income, private equity and structured products. AFC’s footprint – a head office in Hong
Kong, a representative office in Vietnam and dedicated funds for Iraq and Uzbekistan – significantly
broadens CAL’s reach and future growth prospects. Its reputation and 13-year track record of investing
across frontier markets in Asia will strengthen CAL’s credibility as we seek to enter new territories.

As we enter the closing stages of 2026, we look forward to 2027 with hope for a more stable and peaceful
world. I am grateful to all our stakeholders for their continued confidence and support.

      Accounting classification and fair value of financial instruments
      Financial instruments measured subsequently on the ongoing basis either at fair value or amortized cost. The summary of significant accounting
      policies describes how the classes of financial instruments are measured, and how income and expenses, including fair value gains and losses,
      are recognized.
      The group measures fair values using the following fair value hierarchy that reflects the significance of inputs used in making the measurement.
      Level - 1
      Financial instruments that are measured in whole or in part by reference to published quotes in an active market. A financial instrument is
      regarded as quoted in an active market if quoted prices are readily and regularly available from an exchange and those prices represent actual
      and regularly occurring market transactions on an arm’s length basis.

      Level - 2
      Financial instruments that are measured at fair value on a recurring basis. As market quotes generally are not readily available or accessible for
      these securities, their fair value measures are determined using relevant information generated by market transactions involving comparable

      Level - 3
      Valuation techniques using significant unobservable inputs. This category includes all instruments where the valuation technique includes inputs
      not based on observable data and the unobservable inputs have a significant effect on the instrument’s valuation. This category includes
      instruments that are valued based on quoted prices for similar instruments where significant unobservable adjustments or assumptions are
      required to reflect differences between the instruments.
