             Group Chief Executive Officer’s Review - Interim Financial Statements
             Dear stakeholder,
             I am pleased to present the Quarterly Financial Review of Capital Alliance Holdings PLC (CALH) for
             the period ended 30th June 2026.
             The Group recorded a loss of LKR 2.3bn for the quarter, of which LKR 1.9bn is attributable to the
             shareholders of CALH. This translates to a loss per share of LKR 0.90. The loss in the past quarter is
             almost entirely an outcome of the ongoing war in Iran and its consequences for interest rates in Sri
             Lanka, which resulted in mark-to-market losses on CAL’s portfolio of Sri Lankan Government
             Securities held in the books of its primary dealing business.
             In response to heavy currency fluctuations arising from the geopolitical tensions, the Central Bank of
             Sri Lanka (CBSL) increased policy rates by 100 basis points (bps) in May-a larger increase than we, or
             the market, had expected. CAL’s primary dealing business derives most of its revenue from trading
             Government securities. Although very lucrative in the long term, this business is adversely affected
             by shocks such as this, as its entire portfolio is marked to market on a daily basis. As a result, the loss
             recorded on the primary dealing business amounted to LKR 2.8bn for the quarter, versus a pre-tax
             profit of LKR 2bn in the corresponding quarter last year. At a Group level, the net loss to equity
             holders of CALH amounted to a lower LKR 1.9bn for the quarter, mainly on account of profits made
             in the other business lines.
             Interest rates don’t stay high forever. What goes up must also come down. The mechanism which
             resulted in a loss in the past quarter works in reverse when interest rates fall.
             CAL’s balance sheet remains strong, liquid, and of a high quality. The Group’s total equity stood at
             just over LKR 20bn as of 30th June 2026.
             The long-term macroeconomic outlook for Sri Lanka remains strong. Government finances are in
             good shape, underpinned by a strong primary surplus. The inflation we are seeing is a result of
             higher energy prices and appears to be transitory. It is worth noting that energy prices do not need
             to come down for inflation to reduce. They simply need to stop increasing. All signs point to a
             recovery in the domestic economy when geopolitical tensions ease.
             The outlook for Bangladesh is also good-both at a macro and micro level. The Bangladesh National
             Party (BNP) secured a strong mandate in elections held earlier this year, and the Government has
             announced sound, coherent plans to significantly strengthen the local economy as well as the capital
             markets. The Government appears to have enlisted the services of extremely well qualified
             Bangladeshis to execute its vision. Combined with the mandate and power it has been given, we
             expect the Bangladeshi economy to perform well in the coming quarters. At a micro level, CAL’s
             presence in Bangladesh over the past four years is gaining traction-we were recently judged the best
             Investment Bank for Markets in Bangladesh by Euromoney. FY27 looks set to be a good year for
             Bangladesh.
             Apart from primary dealing, CAL’s other business units are, on balance, performing well.

             The asset management business is growing rapidly in both Sri Lanka and Bangladesh, with net
             operating income growing 24% YoY, and Assets Under Management (AUM) between Sri Lanka and
             Bangladesh increasing to over LKR 350bn.
             Our investment banking pipeline is extremely strong. We raised over LKR 30bn in the capital markets
             in the quarter under review, with significant transactions on track for the remainder of the year. The
             reported results for investment banking are an outcome of the billing cycle rather than of business
             activity, as deal flow remains strong across both Sri Lanka and Bangladesh.
             Significant profits are also being generated by leveraging on the Group’s balance sheet outside the
             primary dealing business. Reported under Eliminations/Unallocated in our segmental disclosures,
             this unit recorded a profit before tax of LKR 405mn for the quarter, versus LKR 37mn a year ago.
             The picture for stockbroking is mixed. The Dhaka Stock Exchange (DSE) is performing well, with daily
             turnover of close to USD 100mn. CAL Securities Bangladesh is benefiting from this activity, and has
             successfully increased market share, currently ranking in the top quintile of stockbrokers in
             Bangladesh. Daily trading volumes in the Colombo Stock Exchange (CSE) have fallen considerably,
             from a high of USD 25-30mn before the war, to as low as USD 5mn in past weeks. CAL Securities in
             Sri Lanka, despite being one of the largest stockbrokers on the CSE, has seen lower volumes as a
             result. Taken as a whole, the Stock Broking segment recorded a profit before tax of LKR 100mn,
             against LKR 209mn in the corresponding quarter last year. We expect this to pick up once
             geopolitical tensions ease, and the CSE becomes more active once again.
             Business highlights for the quarter include the operationalisation of CAL Global Pte Ltd in Singapore,
             which will act as a key distribution hub for CAL’s transactions in the future, helping unlock large pools
             of capital for clients in frontier markets like Sri Lanka and Bangladesh. CAL now serves over 60,000
             clients through its online portals, growing strongly and on track to double YoY. CAL’s client portals-
             apps, client interfaces as well as the core back-end systems are developed in-house by our
             developers. The value of IP we have created in this area is significant, and will be a key asset as we
             roll out our global expansion plans in the coming quarters. The quarter under review also saw the
             launch of CAL’s first Sharia compliant investment product-the first in a line of Sharia compliant
             products we aim to launch to serve the community of Islamic finance investors in Sri Lanka, and
             which will subsequently be rolled out to Bangladesh.
             CAL’s long-term strategy is on track, and our business outlook is strong. We are looking aggressively
             to expand overseas, to use our balance sheet to create new products and grow revenue, and to
             leverage on technology to build platforms that can improve access to investment banking products
             across the frontier markets we operate in.
             I would like to take this opportunity to thank you all for your continued support as we work towards
             achieving our purpose of using the power of capital markets to create prosperity for the
             communities we serve.

                 Accounting classification and fair value of financial instruments
                 Financial instruments measured subsequently on the ongoing basis either at fair value or amortized cost. The summary of significant accounting policies

                 describes how the classes of financial instruments are measured, and how income and expenses, including fair value gains and losses, are recognized.
                 The group measures fair values using the following fair value hierarchy that reflects the significance of inputs used in making the measurement.

                 Level - 1
                 Financial instruments that are measured in whole or in part by reference to published quotes in an active market. A financial instrument is regarded as
                 quoted in an active market if quoted prices are readily and regularly available from an exchange and those prices represent actual and regularly occurring
                 market transactions on an arm’s length basis.

                 Level - 2
                 Financial instruments that are measured at fair value on a recurring basis. As market quotes generally are not readily available or accessible for these

                 securities, their fair value measures are determined using relevant information generated by market transactions involving comparable securities.

                 Level - 3
                 Valuation techniques using significant unobservable inputs. This category includes all instruments where the valuation technique includes inputs not
                 based on observable data and the unobservable inputs have a significant effect on the instrument’s valuation. This category includes instruments that are
                 valued based on quoted prices for similar instruments where significant unobservable adjustments or assumptions are required to reflect differences
                 between the instruments.
